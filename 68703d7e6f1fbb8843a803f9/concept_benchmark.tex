\begin{abstract}
Concept bottleneck models predict outcomes from high-level concepts detected in inputs. Although concepts provide a simple way to reap benefits from interpretability, very few datasets include concept labels. This limits researchers' ability to determine which problems are suitable for these models, isolate the factors that drive their performance or lead to failures, or uncover which algorithms perform well. In this paper, we develop synthetic benchmarks for concept-bottleneck models, focusing on their two main use cases: decision support, in which models assist humans in making better decisions, and automation, in which models handle routine tasks without supervision. Our benchmarks can generate labeled datasets while controlling for properties that affect performance, including data modality, concept choice, annotation quality, and completeness. We demonstrate how the benchmarks can be used to evaluate major classes of concept bottleneck models. Our demonstrations show how the benchmarks can surface failure modes and inform best practices.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{Sec::Intro}
Many machine learning models are developed for settings in which humans require interpretability to verify, contest, and correct automated decisions. A promising approach to introduce interpretability in these settings is to build models whose predictions are based on \emph{concepts}, i.e., meaningful, high-level attributes of the input. For example, consider an automated melanoma classification task from dermoscopic images. The task is well-established with a plethora of high-performing black-box models~\citep{esteva2017derm, kaur2022melanoma, sm2023classification, adepu2023melanoma, teja2024lightweight}, but their adoption is hindered by the lack of interpretability~\citep{metta2024advancing, chanda2024dermatologist, tonekaboni2019clinicians}. In this application, a concept‑based model could first infer whether there are \textcp{dots} or \textcp{streaks} on the skin (diagnostic cues used by clinicians during screening~\citep{thomas1998semiological, ebell2008clinical}), and then use these concepts to predict the presence of melanoma. Clinicians could verify whether the detected concepts are correct, e.g., whether the image really shows streaks, and whether they influence the prediction in clinically sensible ways, e.g., whether streaks appropriately increase the probability of melanoma.

Concept bottleneck models (CBMs)~\citep{koh2020concept} are machine learning systems that implement this approach. They first predict concepts from raw data, and then use simple models to map concepts to the final label. CBMs have attracted significant interest, with studies covering new architectures, training methods, and intervention strategies~\citep[e.g., see surveys][]{di2025ante, sinha2025comprehensive}.

The most significant barrier to adopting CBMs in practice is that their development requires datasets with concept annotations. 
%
There are few such datasets because developing them is costly: if a task has $m$ concepts, the final dataset will require $m$ times the annotation cost. This scarcity of datasets is a barrier to research progress on two fronts: 

\begin{enumerate}[]
\item \emph{Coverage}: Current evaluations are too narrow and may fail to reveal important failure modes. In standard machine learning, broader benchmarks often surface limitations early enough that we can address them during algorithm design and report them. Here, limited ``dataset coverage'' (see~\cref{Table::DatasetsInLiterature}) sharply limits such reporting.

\item \emph{Use Cases}: Current evaluations do not always reveal where CBMs fail on tasks with clear practical stakes. This is because many commonly used tasks do not make it easy to evaluate whether correcting concepts would improve decisions or reduce human work (e.g., predicting attractiveness from face attributes~\citep{liu2015faceattributes}). In this regime, experiments provide limited guidance on how to improve the model.
\end{enumerate}


In this paper, we present new synthetic benchmarks for CBMs that help address these barriers. Our benchmarks target decision support and automation, and use synthetic data generation to broaden coverage without requiring costly manual concept annotation. Our main contributions are as follows:

 \begin{enumerate}
    
     \item We design benchmark tasks and metrics to reflect use cases where CBMs provide unique value: automating routine tasks measured by a reduction in work (which we study through Sudoku board validation) and supporting human decision-making measured by gain in performance relative to a black-box model (which we study through robot type prediction from visual features).
     %for automation and decision support. These benchmarks allow practitioners 

     \item We introduce dataset generators that quickly produce data for developing CBMs, allowing us to vary modality, concept granularity, annotation quality, and concept-label mapping.

     \item We demonstrate how to use our benchmarks to (1) identify limitations and determine the scope under which CBMs work; (2) test foundational assumptions about CBMs (e.g., what if we do not know the concepts); (3) evaluate how their performance changes under different conditions (e.g., concept noise, alignment constraints).

     % \item We identify new failure modes of CBMs: performance degradation caused by overly specific concept sets and ineffective interventions arising from annotation noise or expert constraints on the model.

     \item We publish our software package \repourl{}.
 \end{enumerate}


\paragraph{Related Work}


Our work is related to a stream of research on concept bottleneck models \citep{koh2020concept}. %
This body of work is broadly motivated by the potential to build interpretable models for tasks for which we would otherwise develop black-box models. %
The idea of adding bottlenecks to black-box models has inspired a diverse range of follow-up work on architectures~\citep[e.g., ][]{ismail2024concept, zintgraf025selective, jeon2025localitycbm, kim2024eqcbm, chowdhury2024adacbm, zhang2024decoupling, hu2024semisupervised, zarlenga2023tabcbm, steinmann2025object, wang2025mvp, hou2024concept}, training strategies~\citep[e.g.,][]{hegde2024improving, penaloza2025preference, selvaraj2024improving, yu2025language, qi2024vip, kim2023concept, sheth2023auxiliary, pang2024integrating} and extensions, e.g., to causal reasoning \citep{dominici2024causal, de2025causally}. Researchers have also studied properties CBMs exhibit with respect to concept specification, such as concept leakage \citep[e.g.,][]{havasi2022addressing, sun2024eliminating}, robustness \citep{hu2025stable, yan2023robust, park2025analysis, lai2024cat, raman2024concept}, or uncertainty quantification \citep{kim2023probabilistic, collins2023human, feng2024bayesian, gao2024evidential}. An important line of work investigated the benefits of using CBMs, including intervention strategies \citep[e.g.,][]{shin2023closer, mahinpei2021promises, shen2025adaptive, balloli2024they, espinosa2024learning, sheth2022learning}, and learning with weak supervision \citep[e.g.,][]{oikarinen2023label, hu2024semisupervised, zintgraf025selective, rao2024discover, xu2025concept, makonnen2025measuring, parisini2025leakage}. %

The literature evaluates CBMs on a range of concept-annotated datasets (see~\cref{Table::DatasetsInLiterature}), including \textds{CUB-200}~\citep{wah2011cub}, \textds{CelebA}~\citep{liu2015faceattributes}, and \textds{CheXpert}~\citep{irvin2019chexpert}. These datasets were not originally designed for concept supervision. For instance, it is unclear which concepts included in these datasets are truly relevant for the task. It is also unclear how noisy concept labels are, and whether the concept set is complete. The high cost of building concept datasets motivated work in concept discovery~\citep[e.g.,][]{rao2024discover, schrodi2024concept, panousis2024coarse, xie2025discovering}, and led many CBM papers to use datasets where concepts and labels are defined via large language models~\citep[as in, e.g.,][]{kim2023probabilistic, oikarinen2023label, yu2025language, yang2023language, zhang2025attribute, he2025v2c, jiang2025enhancing}. This procedure can introduce noise into concept annotations that is difficult to quantify. As a result, the existing datasets do not provide the level of control to study CBMs under all relevant conditions e.g., comparing how the models fare when not all relevant concepts are annotated. The evaluations may also confound shortcomings in the model architecture with imperfections in the data.

Our work seeks to complement research on CBMs with controlled synthetic benchmarks. Current synthetic datasets for concept-based models remain limited~\citep[e.g., ][]{pugnana2025deferring, shin2023closer, alukaev2023cross, barbiero2024relational, makonnen2025measuring, parisini2025leakage}, are rarely available for public use, and are often built ad hoc for specific questions. Established benchmarks in machine learning~\citep[see e.g.,][]{johnson2017clevr, ding2021retiring, schwettmann2023find, grinsztajn2022tree, koh2021wilds} provide: (i) clear evaluation targets, (ii) controlled variation to make comparisons across models and training settings; (iii) access to ground truth, (iv) reproducibility.
Synthetic datasets support these properties because they are generated under fully specified conditions. These advantages are especially important for concept-based models, because evaluation hinges on isolating the effect of concept learning on downstream performance.


\newsection{Use Cases}
\label{Sec::Preliminaries}
We provide an overview of concept bottleneck models and formally define automation and decision support as their canonical use cases.

\paragraph{Preliminaries}
We consider a supervised learning task where we predict a label from a complex feature space. We start with a dataset of $n$ examples $\{(\features_i, \concepts_i, \outcome_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, where $\features_{i} \in \X \subseteq \R^d$ are features (e.g., pixels), $\concepts_{i} \in \C = \{0, 1\}^m$ are \emph{concepts} (e.g., $\concepts_{i,k} = 1$ if image $i$ shows a lesion with \textcp{streaks}), and $\outcome_{i} \in \Y = \{0, 1\}$ is a \emph{label} (e.g., patient $i$ has melanoma)\footnote{We focus on binary classification; the formalism extends to multi-class settings}. 

We use the dataset to train:
%
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin =*]
    \item A set of $m$ \emph{concept detectors} $\detector_k: \X \to [0, 1]$ that predict the probability the concept is present $\Pr{\concept_{i, k} = 1 \mid \features_i}.$ 
    
    \item A \emph{front-end model} $\frontend: \C \to [0,1]$, which maps concepts $\concepts_i$ to the \emph{soft} label $\frontend(\concepts_i)$.
\end{itemize}
%
The full CBM pipeline $\pipeline: \X \to \Y$ outputs the \emph{hard} label $\outcomepred_i$ by thresholding the soft label.

%Concept bottleneck models can be trained using various approaches~\citep[e.g.,][]{selvaraj2024improving, qi2024vip, kim2023concept, sheth2023auxiliary}. %In what follows, we focus on an independent training setup where we train concept detectors and the front-end model separately. In an image classification task, the concept detector $\detector_k$ would be a DNN fit on dataset $\{(\features_i, \concepts_{i,k})\}_{i=1}^n$, and the front-end model $\frontend$ would be a logistic regression model fit on $\{(\concepts_i, \outcome_i)\}_{i=1}^n$. This is a broadly applicable approach that overcomes barriers to adoption because we can train concept detectors on partial data (only examples with relevant concept annotations) and train them by fine-tuning an existing DNN~\citep{mertk2022posthoc,joren2024classification}.


\paragraph{Interventions}

One practical benefit of training a concept bottleneck is that semantically meaningful concepts provide a path to improve models through human \emph{intervention}:
%
\begin{enumerate}
    
    \item \emph{Confirmation}: A user corrects the predicted concept. For example, if a detector predicts that concept $k = \textcp{dots}$ is present in image $i$ with unmarked skin, $\conceptspred_{i,k} = 1$, the user can correct this by setting $\conceptspred_{i,k} = 0$ and using the new value in the front-end model.
    
    \item \emph{Manipulation}: A user edits the raw input to toggle a concept. If the detector fails to detect \textcp{dots} in image $i$, the user can digitally enhance them so the detector picks them up.
    
\end{enumerate}

The value of interventions depends on the concepts and instances to which humans are asked to attend. 
In what follows, we consider a general strategy based on the responsiveness of the label to changes in concept values. We chose this approach over alternatives~\citep{shin2023closer,shen2025adaptive} because it maximizes effectiveness while accounting for concept uncertainty. 

An intervention induces a mapping $t: \C \times \X \to\C$ that, given input $\features$, modifies a subset of selected concepts $S(\features) \subseteq \{1,\ldots,m\}$ before they are passed to the front-end model. We denote the number of intervened concepts by $k = |S(\features)|$.

We quantify the benefit of interventions as the improvement in expected performance with respect to a performance metric. For accuracy, this improvement is given by:
\begin{align}
\incacc(\pipeline) = \E[\frontend(t(\detector(\features), \features)) = \outcome] - \E[\frontend(\detector(\features)) = \outcome] 
\end{align}

\subsection{Decision Support}
Decision support encompasses specialized tasks where models assist experts in making decisions under uncertainty, often in settings with probabilistic or unknown ground-truth labels~\citep[see, e.g., diagnostic applications][]{kawahara2018derm,irvin2019chexpert}. 
%
The overarching motivation for using machine learning in this use case is to improve the accuracy of decisions. 

The simplest approach is to build models that outperform experts~\citep{grove2000clinical, aegisdottir2006meta}. These models cannot be easily analyzed to understand their predictions and are therefore difficult to collaborate with. Concept bottleneck models, on the other hand, are well-suited for collaboration. We may accompany predictions with explanations~\citep[see][]{arrieta2020explainable, belle2021principles, adadi2018peeking} derived from the front-end model to help experts decide when to rely on the model's predictions. We may also have experts validate concept predictions and delegate the downstream task to the model, since it is more accurate. Experts can also pass domain knowledge onto the front-end model $\frontend$ to align its predictions with domain standards.

CBMs become viable to the extent that they outperform black-box alternatives with interventions. Unlike $\incacc$, which measures the benefit of interventions alone, we benchmark concept-based models by their change in performance after interventions relative to a baseline predictor $\baseline$:
\begin{align}
\gain(\pipeline, \baseline) =  \E[\frontend(t(\detector(\features), \features)) = \outcome] - \E[\baseline(\features) = \outcome] .
\label{Def::Gain}
\end{align}

  
\subsection{Automation}

One of the most powerful uses of concept-based models is for automation~\citep{joren2024classification}. These tasks represent a major use case for machine learning models in domains that require large volumes of repeated work. For example, government agencies process billions of non-digital documents annually~\citep{google2022documentai} (e.g., taxes, immigration, benefits) and use models to check if these documents contain responses/signatures for all mandatory sections~\citep[][]{pandey2023ai, di2024streamlining, gruzauskas2020robotic, shende2024enhancing}. 

Standard models for these tasks, called selective classifiers, automate the ``easy" cases and defer uncertain instances to human experts~\citep[see][]{el2010foundations, geifman2017selective, gangrade2021selective}. These models abstain on uncertain predictions according to threshold $\threshold \in [0, 0.5]$, i.e., when $\pipeline(\features) \in [\threshold, 1-\threshold]$, the model outputs $\abstain$ (abstain) rather than a label. Their performance is evaluated on the subset of instances they choose to predict using \emph{selective accuracy}:
%
\begin{align}
    \textsf{SelectiveAccuracy}(\pipeline) = \E[\pipeline(\features) = \outcome \mid \pipeline(\features) \neq \abstain]
\end{align}
%
The proportion of instances where they predict is called \emph{coverage}:
%
\begin{align}
    \textsf{Coverage}(\pipeline) = \E[\pipeline(\features) \neq \abstain]
\end{align}
%

Resolving model abstentions is costly because it requires manual inspection of all instances on which the model abstains. Concept-based models can improve automation and reduce that cost by allowing interventions: rather than having humans review entire instances, they can identify when human input is needed and specify concepts that require verification~\citep{joren2024classification}. To compute ``safe" automation, we restrict attention to models that satisfy a fixed selective accuracy requirement (see \cref{Fig::AccuracyCoverage} and \citep{geifman2017selective}). 
%
%
We quantify automation using a net utility metric, which captures the fraction of work handled automatically after accounting for the human effort required by interventions:
%
\begin{align}
    \wrk(\pipeline) = \E[\pipeline(\features) \neq \abstain] - \E\!\left[\frac{|S(\features)|}{m}\right]
\end{align}
%
A high value of this metric ($\wrk(\pipeline)\approx 1$) indicates very few interventions that produce a relatively low human cost can yield a substantial increase in automated work whereas a low value ($\wrk(\pipeline)\approx 0$) indicates that interventions fail to resolve abstentions and instead introduce additional human effort without commensurate gains in automation.

We present a benchmark for decision-support tasks, i.e., tasks in which experts use models to improve the accuracy of their own uncertain decisions.

\paragraph{Task}

We consider the task of classifying fictional robots known as Glorps and Drents. Our task is inspired by \citet{williams2010role}, who used these robots to study how explanations can help humans discover new concepts~\citep[see also][for other adaptations]{skirzynski2024overreliance, skirzynski2025discrimination}. This task models decision support well because we control the ground-truth labeling function and the relevant concepts, yet we can evaluate CBMs under realistic conditions in which this knowledge is hidden from the model.

For this task, $\features$ are pixels in an image of a robot, and concepts $\concepts$ describe the shape or presence of salient body parts (see~\cref{Fig::RobotFeatures}):

\end{align*}

The label $\outcome \in \{0, 1\}$ denotes robot type (Glorp or Drent).

This yields 7,680 distinct robot instances. We can use these to create up to 921{,}600 robot instances by rendering each robot with one of 120 different color schemes.

\noindent \emph{Concept Relevance}: We can set the ground truth labelling function in terms of concepts. Beyond these examples, we can also include a tabular representation which we can use to evaluate how models can perform if we were given ground-truth annotations.

\paragraph{Task}

% What makes Sudoku the best benchmark for automation
We consider the task of determining if a Sudoku board is \emph{valid} -- i.e., contains the digits 1-9 exactly once in each row, column, and block. Sudoku validation provides a strong baseline for automation because we can measure $\wrk$ precisely by counting the number of concepts that require human confirmation. 

In a standard Sudoku board, a $9 \times 9$ grid is divided into $\nBoard{} \times \nBoard{} = 3 \times 3$ non-overlapping sub-grids. We have to check the validity of $3\nBoard{}^2 = 27$ rules: $9$ rules for each row, column, and block. We build a concept-based model with concepts \begin{align*}
\crow{j} &:= \indic{\text{$j$-th row contains all numbers exactly once}}, \\
\ccol{j} &:= \indic{\text{$j$-th column contains all numbers exactly once}}, \\
\cblk{j} &:= \indic{\text{$j$-th block contains all numbers exactly once}}.
\end{align*}

We predict the final label as the logical AND of the concepts:
\begin{align}
    y & = \mathrm{AND}(\ccol{1}, \ldots, \ccol{\nBoard{}^2},\ldots, \crow{\nBoard{}^2},\ldots, \cblk{\nBoard{}^2}) \notag \\
      &= \prod_{\substack{j=1,\ldots,\nBoard{}^2}} \crow{j}\ccol{j} \cblk{j}
\label{Eq::SudokuDomain}
\end{align}

In this section, we demonstrate how synthetic benchmarks can support model development by revealing failure modes and exploring untested assumptions. We provide additional details in \cref{Appendix::Demonstrations} and code to reproduce our experiments in \repourl{}.


\paragraph{Setup}
\label{subsec::setup}

We generate two benchmarks:
\begin{enumerate}
    
    \item \emph{Automation}: We consider an instance of the \textds{sudoku} benchmark that reflects the inherent traits of automation tasks -- i.e., humans can identify and annotate ground truth concepts, models may not be able to detect them perfectly, and concept-based models provide a way to improve coverage by asking humans to confirm uncertain concepts. We generate a dataset of 1,000 $9\times 9$ Sudoku boards, with balanced validity (50\% valid, 50\% invalid). We render each board as a 454 x 454 px image, with typeset starting values, handwritten entries, and candidate values scattered across a random subset of cells. %In this setting, %concepts through interventions always concerned $k=1, 3, \text{max}$ most uncertain concepts. 

    \item \emph{Decision Support}: We consider an instance of the \textds{robots} benchmark where we can study how the performance and effectiveness of interventions in concept-based models depend on the ability of human experts to identify and annotate concepts. In this case, we compare models on two datasets: \textds{true\_concepts}, where we have ground-truth concepts, and \textds{human\_concepts}, where we train models using concepts that are recognized and annotated by human experts. We consider a task in which the labeling function is $y = f(c_1,\dots,c_7)$, where $c_k$ denotes a body-part concept. We assume that experts correctly define a concept for each body part, as these are salient characteristics that vary across images. We control the deviation between ground-truth and what humans infer by varying \textcp{FootShape} as shown in \cref{Fig::ConceptDiscovery}. In \textds{true\_concepts}, each \textcp{FootShape} is a binary feature that captures high-level values \textcp{Pointy} and \textcp{Flat}. In \textds{human\_concepts}, \textcp{FootShape} is a categorical feature encoded in terms of the distinct subtypes, with an uneven distribution of subtypes. To study how each architecture responds to automated concept annotation, we additionally test three concept sources where labeling is performed by an LLM (GPT-4o-mini):  \textds{machine\_annotation} (the same concepts as \textds{human\_concepts}), \textds{llm\_concepts} (concepts generated by an LLM, such as ``Gradient color'' and ``Square hands''), and \textds{clip\_concepts} (concepts from the CLIP-Dissect vocabulary~\citep{oikarinen2022clip}, such as ``transistor'', ``robotic'', and ``physics''). Finally, we vary who performs interventions at test time: an oracle (\textds{perfect}), a human expert with 80\% accuracy (\textds{expert}), or an LLM that judges concepts from robot images (\textds{llm}).

\end{enumerate}

We divided each dataset into training and test samples. We used the training sample to train models and tune their parameters, and the test sample to evaluate performance on the metrics listed in \cref{Sec::Preliminaries}. 

We test four established concept-based architectures that each address different failure modes of CBMs, and show the ability of our benchmarks to surface when these architectures are not suitable and why. Our baseline concept-based model is the standard \CBM{}~\citep{koh2020concept}, which predicts binary concepts and maps them to labels through a logistic regression frontend~\citep{mertk2022posthoc,joren2024classification}. We also test \CEM{}~\citep{zarlenga2022concept}, which replaces binary concept outputs with learned per-concept embeddings to preserve richer representations. 
%During training, \CEM{} randomly replaces some predicted concepts with ground-truth values so the label predictor is exposed to both predicted and corrected inputs, reducing the distribution shift that occurs when a human intervenes at test time. 
Then, we test \ProbCBM{}~\citep{kim2023probabilistic}, which models concepts as Gaussian latent variables so the model can express per-concept uncertainty. 
%This allows the label predictor to weigh uncertain concepts less, rather than treating a noisy $0.51$ prediction the same as a confident $0.99$. 
Finally, we test \ECBM{}~\citep{xu2024energy}, which uses energy-based scoring to train concept detection and label prediction jointly. 
%This avoids the mismatch that arises in independently trained CBMs, where the label predictor is trained on ground-truth concept values but receives noisy predicted values at test time. 
For automation, we adapt all four architectures as selective classifiers~\citep{joren2024classification}. As a black-box baseline, we trained a deep neural network (\DNN{}) end-to-end on each task.

\paragraph{Validation of the Benchmark on Decision Support}
\label{Sec::DecisionSupportValidation}

In this section, we show that our benchmarks can differentiate concept-based architectures and diagnose why some of them fail. We compare all four architectures on \textds{human\_concepts} with perfect interventions (see \cref{tab:robot-big-table}).

The \DNN{} baseline achieves $87.5\%$ by predicting the majority class; it cannot find signal beyond the class prior. Because we know the labeling rule, we can examine accuracy on the three concepts that drive the label --- \textcp{MouthType}, \textcp{HasKnees}, and \textcp{FootShape} --- to diagnose why some architectures benefit from interventions and others do not.

On \textds{human\_concepts} with perfect interventions, \CBM{}, \CEM{}, and \ProbCBM{} all improve with interventions, while \ECBM{} is flat at $87.5\%$ for partial budgets and only recovers at $k{=}\text{max}$ (see \cref{tab:robot-big-table}). \CBM{} improves from $79.9\%$ to $91.2\%$ at $k{=}3$ and reaches $94.9\%$ at $k{=}\text{max}$; \CEM{} reaches $94.2\%$ at $k{=}1$ and $94.0\%$ at $k{=}\text{max}$; \ProbCBM{} reaches $89.0\%$ at $k{=}3$ and $94.9\%$ at $k{=}\text{max}$. \ECBM{}'s concept detector learns a majority-class template on the three key concepts --- predicting all of them as $0$ for drents (the minority class). Because these predictions appear confident, the intervention policy does not select them for correction, so interventions at partial budgets leave the relevant concepts unchanged. Only at $k{=}\text{max}$, when all concepts are corrected, does accuracy recover to $92.8\%$.

We examine \ProbCBM{} more closely because it achieves the highest accuracy at $k{=}\text{max}$. We hypothesize that this stems from its sequential training and explicit representation of per-concept uncertainty --- a design choice advocated by~\citet{kim2023probabilistic} so that concept embeddings remain focused on representing uncertainty rather than encoding shortcuts around the bottleneck. We can test this claim directly: when we train \ProbCBM{} jointly and perform the same analysis, we find that accuracy is flat at $87.5\%$ across all budgets. Examining the concept predictions, the key concepts are predicted at chance (${\approx}50\%$), which is why interventions fail. Leakage metrics~\citep{parisini2025leakage} confirm the bottleneck is bypassed (\cref{Table::Leakage}). We note that \CEM{} is also trained jointly but avoids this failure through its RandInt mechanism, which presents the label predictor with a mix of predicted and ground-truth concept values during training. Our benchmark enables these comparisons because we know which concepts drive the label and can directly verify whether each architecture learns them.

\paragraph[Robustness to concept specification]{\mbox{Robustness to concept specification.}}
\label{Sec::ConceptSpecification}

Practitioners cannot know the true generative concept set for their task. They annotate the concepts they can see, which are often finer-grained than the latent structure that drives the label. Our benchmark can stress-test whether a method survives this mismatch because we can swap the concept set while holding the images, labels, and labeling rule fixed.

We compare all four architectures on \textds{true\_concepts} (7 binary concepts matching the generative rule) and \textds{human\_concepts} (12 finer-grained subconcepts) under perfect interventions (see \cref{tab:robot-big-table}).

\CEM{} is robust: its baseline is comparable on both concept sets ($84.9\%$ and $87.3\%$), and interventions yield gains in both cases ($95.3\%$ at $k{=}3$ on \textds{true\_concepts}; $94.2\%$ at $k{=}1$ on \textds{human\_concepts}).

\CBM{} and \ProbCBM{} are both sensitive to concept specification: their baselines and intervention ceilings drop with finer-grained concepts, but interventions help in both cases. For example, \CBM{} drops from $97.1\%$ ($k{=}3$) on \textds{true\_concepts} to $91.2\%$ on \textds{human\_concepts}; \ProbCBM{} drops from $94.3\%$ to $89.0\%$.

\ECBM{} reveals the most severe sensitivity. On \textds{true\_concepts}, it benefits substantially from interventions ($86.4\%$ to $95.2\%$ at $k{=}3$) --- comparable to \CBM{} and \CEM{}. On \textds{human\_concepts}, it is flat at $87.5\%$ for all partial budgets. Where \CBM{} and \ProbCBM{} degrade gracefully with finer concepts, \ECBM{} fails catastrophically.

Our benchmark surfaces distinct patterns under concept specification mismatch, ranging from graceful degradation (\CBM{}, \ProbCBM{}) to robustness (\CEM{}) to catastrophic sensitivity (\ECBM{}). These patterns are invisible on real datasets because no real dataset supports swapping the concept set while holding the task fixed.



\paragraph{Robustness to Concept Source}
\label{Sec::Noise}

\begin{wrapfigure}[18]{R}{0.7\linewidth}
    \vspace{-1em}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth, page=24]{figures/figures.pdf}
    \caption{Mean $\Delta$ Accuracy (averaged over $k \in \{1, 3, \text{max}\}$) across concept source and intervention pipelines. \CBM{} benefits from hand-annotated concepts but backfires on automated ones; other architectures are flat or recover toward the majority-class baseline.}
    \label{fig:relative-change}
\end{wrapfigure}

Concept annotation is expensive, and practitioners increasingly turn to automated concept discovery~\citep{oikarinen2023label, oikarinen2022clip}. Our benchmark can stress-test whether intervention benefit survives with automated concepts, because we can vary the concept source and intervention source independently while holding the task fixed (see \cref{tab:robot-big-table} and \cref{fig:relative-change}).

\CBM{} benefits from interventions on hand-annotated concepts ($+1\%$ to $+12\%$) but backfires on automated ones ($-13\%$ to $-49\%$). At $k{=}0$, \CBM{} predicts the majority class on automated concepts --- the concept predictions carry little task-relevant signal. Intervening activates the front-end's concept coefficients at full strength, but since the automated concepts do not relate to the task, these coefficients produce noise rather than signal~\citep[see also][]{shin2023closer, park2025analysis}.

\CEM{} and \ProbCBM{} are both degraded by automated concepts at $k{=}0$: their baseline accuracy drops below the majority-class rate (e.g., \CEM{} drops to $81.7\%$ on \textds{llm\_concepts}; \ProbCBM{} drops to $71.0\%$ on \textds{clip\_concepts}). The automated concept values actively push predictions in the wrong direction. The two architectures differ in how they respond to interventions: \ProbCBM{} recovers to the majority-class rate ($87.5\%$) --- correcting the concept values neutralizes the damage but does not improve beyond the prior, because the corrected values are accurate but irrelevant to the task. \CEM{} does not recover ($81.7\% \to 81.6\%$ at $k{=}\text{max}$); the damage from automated concepts persists even after correction.

\ECBM{} is flat at $87.5\%$ across all concept sources (\cref{Sec::DecisionSupportValidation}).

Our benchmark surfaces a failure mode that is invisible on real datasets: automated concepts that do not align with the task can degrade baseline accuracy, cause interventions to backfire, or both. These effects cannot be detected without varying concept source quality independently of the task.

    \label{Fig::robot_alignment}
\end{wrapfigure}

Concept-based models allow practitioners to encode domain knowledge by constraining concept weights in the frontend, for example by fixing the sign of a feature weight to match clinical expectations~\citep{joseph2025cognitive, adler2022meeting}. Our benchmark can stress-test whether such constraints survive training, because we know the true concept weights and can measure intervention gain on a constrained concept before and after the constraint is applied.

We set the weight on \textcp{HasKnees} to zero. This choice appears reasonable: knees have poor detector accuracy ($\approx 50\%$) and experts could easily miss their predictive importance, as knees appear commonly in both classes. The constraint preserves training accuracy ($88\%$ to $88.1\%$), so a practitioner inspecting only accuracy would accept it. However, as shown in \cref{Fig::robot_alignment}, the constraint destroys the benefit of interventions. On \textds{human\_concepts}, the unconstrained \CBM{} outperforms the \DNN{} by $6.9\%$ with interventions, whereas the constrained \CBM{} \emph{underperforms} by $8\%$. The same pattern appears on \textds{true\_concepts} ($+10.2\%$ vs.\ $-0.3\%$). Training accuracy is preserved because gradient descent routes task-relevant signal through the unconstrained concepts to compensate for the fixed weight. This failure mode is visible on our benchmark because we can measure intervention gain on a concept with a known true weight. On a real dataset, training accuracy alone would suggest the constraint is harmless.

\label{Sec::AutomationValidation}

\begin{wraptable}[18]{R}{0.55\linewidth}
    \centering
    \resizebox{0.49\textwidth}{!}{
    \small
    \input{tables/sudoku_tab}
    }
    \caption{$\textsf{SelectiveAccuracy}$, $\textsf{NetWorkAutomated}$, and $\textsf{Coverage}$ on a real-world \textds{ppe} checklist task and on our synthetic \textds{sudoku} benchmark. The architecture ranking changes across datasets. \CEM{} leads on \textds{ppe} ($73.0\%$), while \CBM{} leads on \textds{sudoku} ($59.0\%$) and \ProbCBM{} falls to $1.0\%$.     \label{Table::sudoku}
\end{wraptable}

The automation benchmark checks whether rankings from a real checklist task hold when the label rule is known. On \textds{ppe}, \CEM{} covers the most cases ($73.0\%$), followed by \ECBM{} ($35.5\%$), \CBM{} ($27.7\%$), and \ProbCBM{} ($17.0\%$). The \DNN{} reaches $54.6\%$. These results rank methods, but they do not explain the ranking. A coverage gap can come from concept detector quality, calibration, bottleneck leakage, concept-label mismatch, label noise, or the architecture itself. \textds{ppe} does not separate these causes.

On \textds{sudoku}, the ranking changes. \CBM{} leads with $59.0\%$ \wrk{}, followed by \ECBM{} ($47.0\%$), \CEM{} ($41.5\%$), and \ProbCBM{} ($1.0\%$). The \DNN{} reaches $10.0\%$. Every label is known because a board is valid only when all $27$ row, column, and block constraints hold. This rule makes \CBM{} a natural fit. Its binary bottleneck and logistic frontend can represent the thresholded sum that implements the AND rule. \ProbCBM{} accepts almost no cases in this setting, which flags a bottleneck question like the one in \cref{Sec::DecisionSupportValidation}. That is the value of the controlled task. \textds{ppe} tells us which method handled more cases. \textds{sudoku} narrows the cause of failure because the rule and every label are known instead of mixed with real-world noise.

\newsection{Concluding Remarks}
\label{Sec::ConcludingRemarks}

%Our goal is to support researchers who wish to understand how models operate, how they may fail before committing to costly data collection or deployment.

In this work, we introduce synthetic benchmarks to support research on concept-based models. Our benchmarks pertain to simple, fictional tasks, in which researchers can readily generate a broad range of datasets, quickly train models, and communicate their findings to a broader audience. Our experiments highlight how we can benefit from such synthetic tasks on several fronts: by expanding the testbed of datasets used to evaluate algorithms, by exploring the impact of untestable assumptions, and by drawing attention to the appropriate performance metrics. Our aim is to provide diagnostic tools rather than a leaderboard. Failures on controlled synthetic tasks are informative: they signal vulnerabilities that are likely to persist or worsen in real-world settings. In this way, our benchmarks help practitioners decide what to investigate further before committing to costly data collection or deployment.

% \paragraph{Limitations and Future Work}
% Our benchmarks focus on tabular and image-based settings and rely on simplified generative assumptions that do not capture the full heterogeneity of real-world data. Extending these tools to additional modalities and richer forms of noise remains an important direction for future work. Future work could also test newer architectures developed to address specific limitations of CBMs, such as concept noise, uncertainty, and selective abstention~\citep{vandenhirtz2024scbm,singhi2024realignment,penaloza2025cpo, pugnana2025deferring}.

\paragraph{Broader Impacts}
Our work introduces benchmarks that support the development of reliable concept bottleneck models by providing researchers with tools that can improve algorithm design, reveal failure modes, and communicate the benefits of concept-based systems. Our benchmarks are designed to delineate conditions under which concept-based models are viable alternatives to standard neural networks for canonical use cases.
